The Real Vibe Coding Era Was Before AI

What if real vibe coding mostly belonged to the pre-AI era?

Vibe coding did not begin with AI. In hindsight, it also describes decades in which we built software with a great deal of implicit knowledge, compressing analysis, design, and part of our documentary rigor inside our own heads just to keep shipping. AI agents do not make the profession easier: they finally make software engineering practicable at scale in places where we often bypassed it for lack of time.

The paradox that took me time to accept

I long believed that I was, first and foremost, a programmer. With some distance, I would now put it differently: I was solving real-world problems by means of code. And that is precisely why today I write very little code myself.

When I do, it is mostly to learn a concept on purpose, by choice. For the rest, I let my coding agents do the work, because they are now far more competent than I am on that terrain.

Put that way, the claim sounds provocative. And yet it is probably the most honest summary of what I have been living through since May 2025, with Ezkey as my personal laboratory.

Like many people, I began with the wonder of AI-assisted coding: asking for a few segments, correcting, adjusting, iterating, and watching classes and tests appear from an intention expressed in natural language. Then I raised the level: less line-by-line coding, more analysis, more structure, more context, more intention — a shift I had already begun describing in From Code to Intent.

For a while, that shift gave me a strange feeling: the feeling of being dispossessed of my own work. If I am no longer the one writing the code, then what exactly am I now? The rise in competence of coding agents hit me right there. I was a programmer. I thought that was the stable core of my professional identity. And suddenly that identity was becoming the starting point of an almost total delegation.

With some hindsight, I think the paradox is simpler than it first appears. If I have always loved computing, it was never for the abstract beauty of typing characters into a keyboard. It was to make systems exist — systems that behave, respond, and solve something. Code was the most direct way to get there. Today, that way is not necessarily mine anymore.

I had learned software engineering. Like everyone else, I mostly learned how to compress it

When I was a teenager, in the 1980s, I was fascinated by something very simple: writing a few lines of Basic or assembly, launching the program, and then watching the machine continue without me. That sense of magic grabbed me strongly enough that I turned it into a field of study, and then a career.

Like anyone seriously trained in the field, I did not only learn how to code. I learned use cases, analysis, sequence diagrams, state diagrams, test plans, interface specifications, flow descriptions — in short, everything that was supposed to let us design before we built.

But in my lived experience as a young programmer, all of that remained a sort of antechamber. Analysis was a way of telling the story of the system before it existed. Design was a kind of more respectable pseudocode. Diagrams helped clarify ideas. Deep down, what I was really waiting for was the moment when we would finally get to write code, more code, and then even more code.

And once the real work began, the old pattern almost always came back. We discovered missed details. We adjusted. We improvised locally. The code moved forward. The documentation, meanwhile, began drifting almost immediately. Pretending we would keep it perfectly aligned often amounted to wishful thinking. At best, we managed for a while. Then the level of obsolescence rose slowly but steadily.

This is not an individual confession of failure. It is a collective way of working that I saw throughout my career. We had learned software engineering. Then, under pressure from time, alignment, budgets, and delivery, we learned to compress it inside our own heads. That is really the direct continuation of what I had already described in Back to Foundations.

What I now call the real vibe coding

That is where my perspective changed.

The term “vibe coding” was given a very precise and often pejorative meaning: throw a few sentences at an AI, hope it inhales an entire application, and then live with the consequences. That definition remains valid. It describes a dangerous kind of lightness.

But now, in October 2026, I allow myself to turn the mirror around.

For most of my career, before truly competent agents arrived, we often worked with a huge amount of implicit knowledge, shortcuts, poorly materialized intention, untracked decisions, uneven documentary quality, and tests that existed without always being tied to a clear vision of what they were actually protecting. In that sense, yes — I now believe I spent most of my career doing a form of vibe coding.

Not because I was working badly.

Not because teams were incompetent.

But because the cognitive and collective cost of keeping all the original practices of software engineering alive, continuously, was enormous — and very rarely fully borne by the industry.

The product owner wants features out on time, for good reasons. The security owner wants a strong enough system at a reasonable cost, for good reasons. Technology upgrades happen in waves because we never really have all the time we would want. Teams have to change a wheel while the car is moving. We introduce a feature flag to secure a deployment, and that is often the right move. But that right move also creates configuration debt, documentary debt, and coherence debt that we only resolve in part.

The industry did not spend fifty years despising software engineering. It mostly learned how to survive without always being able to honor it all the way through.

May 2025: I began by trying to replace the programmer I was

When I started Ezkey as a personal laboratory in May 2025, my first instinct was simple: delegate code.

At first it was modest. A few segments at a time. One session at a time. Many adjustments. Then a full class with a few tests. Then a small feature, with a healthy dose of reorientation. And, as models kept improving, a growing level of autonomy.

With hindsight, I can clearly see what I was trying to do: replace myself as a programmer while still keeping myself in the role of principal programmer. That was bound to be awkward. I micromanaged a great deal. I kept correcting. The results were sometimes impressive, but I remained trapped inside my old mental patterns.

I had difficulty turning my thinking into produced intention. Doing real analysis forced me to relearn something I thought I had known forever. Doing design properly, without falling back into disguised pseudocode, also required a new discipline. For quite a while, I mostly increased my capabilities without giving stable form to what I was learning.

I then did vibe analysis, vibe design, and vibe testing

AI-assisted coding was rising. So was I. But my own evolution was anything but linear.

Between May and October 2025, I mostly lived through a first phase of delegated coding. Then, from October 2025 to spring 2026, I adopted plan mode massively in order to run analyses, prepare designs, and then launch implementation. That elevation was real, but it remained chaotic. I was improvising my way of working almost every time a new analysis began. I made progress, yes, but I also produced results that were at times inconsistent and contradictory.

In other words, I had stopped doing only vibe coding in the narrow sense. I was doing vibe analysis, vibe design, and vibe testing. I had recovered higher-level practices, but without a feedback loop structured enough to make them truly systematic.

Under Ezkey, I produced mountains of material: plans, notes, visions, analyses, trails, essays. It was rich, sometimes fertile, but also chaotic. A fairly faithful reflection of what I was: a more-or-less organized programmer suddenly confronted with the very real possibility of sustaining much more rigor than before.

Going back to foundations was not enough: I also had to avoid reinventing RUP

Starting in April 2026, I wanted to put a more serious frame around that practice.

I thought I had seen the light. In reality, I produced a methodology that was too heavy, with too many steps, too many levels, and a certain temptation to make everything fit into a very structured apparatus. There were good intuitions in it, especially the idea of a value compass to avoid cutting off probabilistic paths too early for agents. But there was also too much procedure.

The irony is that I explicitly thought I was avoiding the reconstruction of a large Rational Unified Process-style methodology from the 2000s.

And yet that is exactly what I was doing.

I had summoned the mirror without really looking at myself in it.

Managing to see that drift was salutary. As models evolved, I was able to perform an ablation, lighten the method, and keep only what truly protected continuity, coherence, and intention. Here again, the lesson matters to me: AI does not justify an inflation of ceremonies. It makes more rigorous practices practicable, but it also forces us to cut what only compensates for our fears. I described that turn more directly in The Ablation Principle.

September 2026: AI Software Factory showed me the real shift in the profession

The clearest turning point, for me, was understanding more clearly what an AI Software Factory actually meant.

At first, I approached it in a nearly anthropomorphic way. I named my bots, assigned them roles, recreated a small team with personalities, responsibilities, and coordination dynamics. It was a useful mental bridge, and at times even amusing. But it was not yet the right abstraction. In hindsight, it also sheds light on what I was already calling the next frontier of intention.

The right abstraction looks far more like a state machine for software work than a miniature human org chart.

An idea becomes a backlog. A backlog feeds a product direction. That direction unfolds into visions. Visions call for analyses. Analyses meet a roadmap, priorities, and phasing. Then come design, stack choices, constraints, and formal ways of validating the qualities being sought. At each level, several agents can contribute, challenge one another, verify coherence, produce, summarize, critique, subdivide, and reorganize.

That is where we finally enter something that feels like fully living software engineering. Not because we are replacing humans, but because the effort needed to keep intention, traceability, critique, documentary coherence, and quality validation alive finally becomes practicable at high frequency. And once that orchestration becomes real, it ends up raising, very concretely, the question of closing the loop — something I explored in Steering by Intention, Closing the Loop.

The profession is not becoming easier. It is becoming more demanding

I think there is a stubborn illusion in the air right now. We sometimes hear that agents will simplify the developer's job. I do not see it that way.

Yes, they simplify access to some forms of execution. Yes, they make certain tasks faster. Yes, they write very good code when intention, constraints, stack choices, conventions, and validation criteria are clearly specified.

But that is precisely where the bar rises.

Because the rise in competence of agents removes an old excuse. We can no longer say so easily: we did not have time to keep analysis alive, design alive, documentary coherence alive, cross-critique alive, testing strategy alive, and intention explicit. These things are becoming more and more affordable. They still require judgment, hierarchy, prioritization, and human arbitration, but they are no longer doomed in advance by their intellectual handling cost.

In that sense, the profession becomes more constraining. And that is normal. The real phase of software engineering, at scale and in a more systematic way, may only be beginning now.

Coding itself should return to agents as much as possible, precisely so that we can sustain the rest at a level worthy of the name.

This realization delights me, but it also tightens something in my chest

There is a less comfortable side to this realization.

When I look back at the end of my career, I also see the dangling threads. Concepts never fully reified. Refactorings postponed until tomorrow because the application was already in production. Structural debts managed in careful small pieces. Tools adopted through proof of concept, then consolidated imperfectly along the way. Migration paths we knew were necessary, without ever really having enough space to close them properly.

I do not dramatize any of this. It is simply the reality of the profession.

But I still cannot help seeing a certain sadness in it: the sadness of having spent years contributing to projects carried by a software engineering posture that was often fragmented, pragmatic, and suboptimal, even when it was honest and competent.

The good news is that the teams who remain will be able to climb that slope more easily than we ever could. They will have agents capable of synthesizing, connecting, critiquing, documenting, challenging, and executing at a very high level. They will have the possibility of becoming genuine software engineers in the fullest sense, not only talented code producers carrying part of the system in their heads.

As for me, I will likely remain above all an active retiree, watching all of this evolve with fascination.

And yet I am rediscovering the wonder of the beginning

There is a beautiful symmetry in all of this nonetheless.

When I started, I was amazed by a few lines of Basic or assembly that could make something happen on the screen without my intervention once launched. Today, the scale has changed, but the wonder is back.

I am no longer interacting with a handful of instructions, but with a set of agents able to help me define a product, refine an analysis, prepare a design, critique a solution, conceive an interface, structure a backlog, validate qualities, and produce a complete deployable system.

I am not living through a mere acceleration of coding. I am living through a renewal of software as a discipline.

And if I push the formula all the way through, here is perhaps the most honest lesson I can draw from my own path:

for me, the real vibe coding era was before AI;

and real software engineering may only be beginning now.
